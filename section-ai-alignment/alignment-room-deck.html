Why ground rules

Rules keep you safe.
Fluency makes you fast.

Most AI use is fine. The risk lives in the exceptions: the client email with personal details pasted into a free tool, the auto-reply that misfires, the answer that sounded confident and was wrong. Ground rules decide once, deliberately, where a person must stay in the loop. Businesses with clear ground rules use AI more, because nobody is guessing.

What best practice looks like

The world has already agreed on the shape.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The international standard for managing AI risk, from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. Four functions: govern (who decides), map (where AI touches your work), measure (test before you trust), manage (routines and the bad-day plan). Our four questions track it exactly.

The backbone of today's session
Australia's responsible AI guidance

The National AI Centre publishes voluntary guidance for Australian organisations adopting AI: decide who is accountable, build AI literacy across the workforce and its leaders, keep humans across important decisions, and be transparent about AI use. Privacy law obligations sit underneath and already apply.

National AI Centre, Guidance for AI Adoption

Everything you draft today fits inside frameworks your board, your auditors and your regulators already recognise. And the good news: for a business your size, it boils down to four questions.

What it all boils down to

Four questions. That's today.

1
Who decides?

One accountable person, named. Ambiguity is the actual risk.

2
Where do we need a human gate?

High stakes and high speed need rules before launch. The rest needs a lighter touch.

3
How do we know it works?

Test before you trust, with your own real examples.

4
What's the plan for a bad day?

Stop, tell, assess, fix. Decided now, not mid-incident.

Those frameworks from the last slide, in working clothes. Knowing them is my job; answering the four questions is yours, and that is what this room does today.

What good looks like · an example for an education provider

A governance statement you can actually remember.

“We use AI to give our students faster answers and our people more time for teaching and care. A person stays accountable for every decision that affects a student's enrolment, progress or wellbeing. Student and staff personal information only goes into tools we have approved. We are open about where AI has shaped what people receive. And we review what our tools are doing every quarter, because this technology changes faster than policies do.”

  • 1Plain words a new starter could repeat. If it needs a lawyer to read it, it will not survive contact with a busy Tuesday.
  • 2It names the lines that do not move: accountability, student data, transparency.
  • 3It commits to review. A dated, owned document beats a perfect one.

And here is the part most businesses miss: this paragraph does double duty. The same words load into your organisation's Claude instructions, so the policy your people read is the policy your AI follows. Ask Claude to do something on your stays-with-a-person list and it will say so, and point you to a person.

This is an example, written for a business like yours. This afternoon you write your own, in your words. It will be better than this one, because it will be true.

Finding the first work

Your time tax, on one grid.

You told us what your week costs you that it shouldn't. Every one of those tasks lands in one of four places.

Stay human-led

Judgement, relationships, and everything on your stays-with-a-person list. AI can support this work; people decide it.

Worth questioning

Work that eats the week but maybe shouldn't exist at all. Fix the process before you buy a tool for it.

AI earns its place

Repeatable, rules-based, easy to check. Strong candidates once the ground rules exist.

Start here

High time tax, low risk, results you can check. This quadrant becomes the first column of your roadmap, and the room chose it, not me.

Two skills make this grid work: delegation, choosing what to hand over, and discernment, judging what comes back. Both are trainable. That is what the sprint builds in your team.
